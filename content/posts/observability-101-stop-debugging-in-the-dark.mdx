## The problem with invisible systems

Picture a logistics company at rush hour. Packages move between warehouses, trucks cross the city, and customers wait. Nobody knows where anything is. There are no tracking numbers, no scan points, and no delivery confirmations. When a customer calls to ask why their package is late, you can only shrug and guess.

Most software systems work exactly this way.

Every time a user clicks a button, a request starts. It travels through APIs, hops between services, queries databases, calls external systems, and eventually produces a response. The user sees the result. But what happened along the way? Where did it slow down? Which service failed?

Without observability, you're running a logistics network blind.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Debugging in the dark

When that customer calls to say their package is late, this is what your day looks like. You phone the warehouse. You radio the driver. You try to reconstruct a journey you never recorded. Maybe you find the problem, and maybe you don't. Mostly, you guess.

Debugging without observability works the same way. You grep through logs. You restart services. You form hypotheses with no data to test them against. Sometimes you get lucky. Often, you're wrong.

The cost is more than time. You lose confidence in your system, in your team, and in your ability to fix things before users notice.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## What observability actually means

Now imagine the opposite. Every package carries a tracking ID, every movement leaves a record, and every delay is logged with a reason.

You open a dashboard and see where the package is, where it slowed down, and what caused the delay. You are looking at what is happening right now, not at yesterday's data or a sample.

Monitoring tells you that something broke. Logging gives you fragments after the fact. Observability is the ability to understand your system's internal state by examining its outputs. It's the difference between "the site is slow" and "Service X took 400ms on this exact trace because the database connection pool was exhausted at 14:32:07."

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## The infrastructure of visibility

Observability doesn't happen by accident. You need a shared format for describing behavior, and a pipeline that collects the signals your services emit and connects them.

This is where OpenTelemetry comes in.

Think of it as the standard that every factory, warehouse, and truck in your logistics network agrees to follow. Every package gets labeled the same way, and every movement gets recorded.

Without this standard, each service records telemetry in its own format. Traces break at service boundaries. Metrics use different dimensions. You end up with isolated fragments that you cannot connect.

With it, you can reconstruct the entire journey of a request.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Inside the factory: the SDKs

Inside each factory, which in this picture is one of your services, workers do the ground-level work. They label packages as they arrive, record timestamps at each stage, and pass tracking information forward so the next worker can continue the chain.

These workers are the OpenTelemetry SDKs. They run inside your application and record what happens as requests pass through it. Without them, there is no tracking and no way to follow a request from its first line of code to its last.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## The central hub: the Collector

In a large logistics network, you don't send every package's tracking data straight to its final destination. You route it through a central hub that receives, sorts, and forwards it.

That's the OpenTelemetry Collector. It receives telemetry from all your services, processes and enriches it, and routes it to the right backends. Your application creates the data. The Collector makes it manageable when many services are sending it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Where the data lives

Different questions need different tools. Here's where each kind of data goes:

- Jaeger stores traces. It shows the complete path of a request through every service it touched.

- Prometheus stores metrics, such as traffic volume, error rates, and latency trends over time.

- Loki stores logs, including raw events, error messages, and the context around them.

Together, they answer the questions you care about. Where did the request go? How is the system behaving? What exactly happened at that moment?

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Three signals, one system

Every observable system reports through three signal types, and each one answers a different question.

### Traces: the journey

"Where did this request go, and how long did each step take?"

Traces capture causality. They show that Service A called Service B, and also when, for how long, and whether that call triggered more work downstream. When you're hunting a latency spike or a partial failure, start with traces.

### Metrics: the big picture

"How is the system behaving overall?"

Metrics are aggregates, such as request rates, error percentages, percentile latencies, and resource usage. They show trends, capacity limits, and the health of your system over time. Alerts fire on metrics, and dashboards are built from them.

### Logs: the details

"What happened at this exact moment?"

Logs record discrete events, such as a thrown error, a user action, or a state change. When a trace tells you where the problem is and a metric tells you when it started, the log tells you why.

Use all three, and correlate them so you can move from a metric spike to the traces behind it, and from a trace to its logs.

## From guessing to knowing

Let's come back to that late package.

Without observability, you guess. You search log files by hand and spend hours reconstructing a timeline that should have been captured automatically.

With observability, you see the route. You find the service where time was lost and the root cause, whether that's a slow database query, a timeout, or a cascading failure, and you fix it.

You stop treating symptoms and start finding causes.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## The bottom line

> A system you cannot see is a system you cannot control.

Observability lets you see what your system is doing. OpenTelemetry gives you a standard way to collect that data across all your services.
